1. How retail fuel prices are actually set in Iraq

Retail fuel prices in Iraq are administratively set, not discovered in a market. The price at the pump is the outcome of three separate decisions that are easy to confuse: the official schedule, the distribution margin, and the transport margin.

The official schedule

Regular and premium gasoline, diesel and kerosene each carry an official retail price per litre. That schedule is the number you see printed on station boards and repeated in the news. It is the reference point every trade calculation starts from, and it changes when the government revises it, not when a station decides.

The distribution layer

Between the refinery gate and the pump sits a distribution margin: the cost of moving product from a depot to stations, plus storage and handling. In practice this is where two traders quoting the same official price end up at different delivered costs, because one is hauling from a nearby depot and the other is moving product across two governorates.

The transport layer

For bulk buyers — generators, factories, contractors, camps — the meaningful unit is not the litre but the load. A tanker has a fixed capacity, a fixed trip cost and a fixed time. That creates a simple and unforgiving arithmetic: the cost per litre falls as the load fills and as the round trip shortens. A half load does not cost half as much.

Practical rule: when someone quotes you a delivered price, ask for three things — the official base price used, the assumed density (for weight-based trade), and the delivery point. Without those three, the quote cannot be compared to another.

Why the published number and the paid number differ

Three common reasons. First, the schedule is a retail figure; wholesale movements rarely settle exactly at it. Second, payment terms change the effective price: cash today and thirty days later are not the same transaction. Third, transport is often quoted separately, so a low product price with expensive haulage can be worse than the reverse. Reading one line of a quotation without the others is the most common source of loss in this business.

2. From litre to tonne: converting a load without losing money

Iraqi fuel is sold both by volume and by weight depending on the counterparty, and that single detail decides whether a deal is profitable or not.

The conversion

The bridge is density. For diesel, the working figure is around 0.83–0.85 kg per litre depending on grade and temperature. For gasoline, roughly 0.72–0.75. Kerosene sits between them.

ProductTypical density1 tonne equals
Diesel0.83–0.85 kg/L≈ 1,176–1,205 litres
Gasoline0.72–0.75 kg/L≈ 1,333–1,389 litres
Kerosene0.78–0.81 kg/L≈ 1,235–1,282 litres

Notice the size of the range: for diesel alone, one tonne moves between roughly 1,176 and 1,205 litres, about 2.5 percent. On a thirty-tonne load, 2.5 percent is three quarters of a tonne of product — often the entire margin.

The rule that prevents the mistake

Fix the density in writing before the load moves, and state which side bears the difference if a measurement disagrees. Weighbridge versus dipstick will never agree exactly; what matters is that the contract says which one governs.

Never convert a quote from weight to volume with an average density after the fact. Convert before you agree, and put the figure in the document.

Temperature

Fuel expands when warm. A load measured at midday in summer and the same load measured at night are not the same volume. For large institutional deliveries, temperature correction is not pedantry; it is the difference between delivering what was invoiced and delivering something else.

3. Margin on one truckload: a worked calculation

This is the arithmetic the calculator on the home page performs. The example uses round numbers so the structure is visible; substitute your own figures.

The inputs

  • Capacity: 30,000 litres.
  • Purchase price: 1,000 per litre (illustrative).
  • Selling price: 1,120 per litre (illustrative).
  • Transport: 1,500,000 for the round trip.
  • Other costs (loading, paperwork, waiting, minor losses): 400,000.

The calculation

Revenue (30,000 × 1,120)33,600,000

Margin on revenue5.06 %
Margin per litre56.7

What the number hides

5 percent looks healthy until three facts are applied. One: the load is not always full — at 27,000 litres with the same trip cost, profit drops sharply because transport is spread over less product. Two: payment delay. If the buyer pays thirty days later, the working capital has a cost that this table does not include. Three: shrinkage and losses in handling, which the "other costs" line above merely approximates.

A margin computed without the trip cost per load and the payment delay is not a margin; it is a hope. Enter both before you quote.

The break-even question

The useful version of this calculation is not "what will I earn" but "at what selling price do I earn nothing". With the figures above, total cost 31,900,000 divided by 30,000 litres gives 1,063.3 per litre. Everything under that number is a loss dressed as a sale.

4. Reading a UNGM tender notice in four checks

Procurement notices on the United Nations Global Marketplace are public and free to read. Most unsuitable ones can be discarded in under two minutes by checking four fields, in this order.

Check one: deadline and timezone

The closing time is stated in a specific timezone, and that timezone is not yours. A notice closing at 14:00 Geneva time closes at 16:00 in Erbil. Reading the hour without the timezone is how offers are lost by two hours on the last day.

Check two: category codes

Each notice carries UNSPSC codes. If your registration does not cover the category, you are usually ineligible regardless of capability. Check the code before you read the scope of work, not after.

Check three: delivery point

For fuel and logistics, the delivery location decides the business. A site in Al-Qaim and a site in Erbil are two different companies' problems. The notice usually states the governorate or the exact warehouse; if it does not, submit a clarification question through the portal.

Check four: evaluation basis

"Lowest price" and "technically compliant" are different competitions. In the second, an incomplete HSE file or a missing company profile can eliminate the cheapest offer. Read the evaluation criteria before writing the financial envelope.

Our own rule: we do not chase a standard goods tender closing in under 48 hours. Preparation cost is real and a rushed submission rarely survives evaluation. Consultancy assignments are the exception — they are shorter by design.

5. Official and market exchange rates: why traders watch both

Iraq operates with an official exchange rate set by the central bank and a market rate that reflects what buyers actually pay. The gap between them is not a curiosity; it moves through every import calculation.

What each rate is for

The official rate is the reference for formal transactions and for the banking channel. The market rate is what a trader meets when settling outside that channel. When the two diverge, the same invoice costs different amounts depending on the route it takes.

Why the gap matters for fuel

Fuel import economics depend on the dollar cost of the product, the customs and transport paid in dinars, and the rate used to convert between them. A one percent gap on a large load is measurable money, and it is the reason two traders with identical product costs can report different results.

How to use both honestly

Show both, label both, and never average them. An average produces a number that exists nowhere and misleads whoever prices against it. On this site, the central bank rate and the market rate are displayed separately with the same timestamp, and if one has not been refreshed, its older date stays visible.

Rule of thumb: price your purchase at the rate you will actually pay, and price your sale at the rate you will actually receive. Do not price both at the more favourable one.
